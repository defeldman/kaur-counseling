[H1] ADHD & Late-Stage Diagnosis.
[P] A diagnosis found in adulthood re-reads an entire life.
[P] An ADHD diagnosis arriving later in life doesn't only name the present. It offers a new language for the past. The relief it can bring is real, and so is the grief that travels beside it. My work is to hold both, and to help you build a life that fits the mind you actually have.
[H2] ADD in women looks different
[P] The picture most people carry is a boy who can't sit still. Women and girls more often live with the inattentive kind: quiet, internal, easy to miss. Daydreaming in class. Losing track of conversations. Holding it together in public, then collapsing at home. Because it hides so well, it's missed for years, and the story becomes "lazy," "scattered," "too sensitive." My clinical focus is women with ADD, and I know its shape intimately.
[P] Relationships
[P] Rejection sensitivity, people-pleasing, losing the thread of what you wanted to say.
[P] Work & career
[P] Capable and praised, then quietly drowning in the details no one sees.
[P] School & study
[P] Bright enough to coast, until you couldn't, and the shame set in.
[P] Self-esteem
[P] A running inner monologue of 'I should have been able to.'
[P] Body & food
[P] Forgetting to eat, then overeating; restless sleep; tension you can't name.
[P] Sex & intimacy
[P] Distracted, disconnected, or running on high alert instead of ease.
[H2] When the diagnosis arrives later
[P] For years you may have moved through the world believing you were simply too much, or never quite enough: too scattered, too intense, too easily overwhelmed, working twice as hard to do the ordinary. A late diagnosis reframes all of it. What you called laziness or brokenness was often a brilliant, exhausting act of holding on.
[H2] How it shows up
[P] A thousand things at once
[P] Ping-ponging between tasks, a mind juggling everything at the same time.
[P] Hyperfocus ↔ overwhelm
[P] Swinging between deep fixation and flood.
[P] Forgetfulness about what you love
[P] Even the things and people that matter slip away.
[P] Time warps and vanishes
[P] Hours pass like minutes, or crawl like days.
[P] The exhaustion of masking
[P] Performing a version of yourself, all day.
[P] The intention-action gap
[P] Knowing exactly what to do, wanting to do it, and feeling paralyzed even when you're smart enough to execute.
[P] 50%+
[P] of adults with ADHD also live with anxiety or depression, and some struggle with both. You are not overreacting; you are responding to a lifetime of feeling unreliable in a world that demanded reliability.
[H2] The grief, and the broken trust in yourself
[P] A late diagnosis often arrives with grief: for the years spent believing you were broken, for the support you never received, for the life that might have felt easier. Living so long without an explanation can erode your trust in your own memory, your follow-through, your word. That fractured trust in yourself can settle into anxiety or depression, which is why so many adults with ADHD carry both.
[P] This fractured trust often creates a cycle of self-frustration: setting high expectations you desperately want to meet, only to find yourself procrastinating or unable to initiate even the simplest tasks. It can be deeply isolating to recognize your own capacity and intelligence while feeling like you're fighting a physical block that keeps you from following through on the routines you know would help you feel better.
[H2] How we work with it
[LI] Honor your gifts: creativity, intensity, range.
[LI] Tend the costs of pretending.
[LI] Grieve what was, and slowly rebuild trust in yourself.
[LI] Build rhythms and boundaries shaped for how your brain actually works.
[P] You are not a problem to be fixed. You are a person learning to live well with yourself.
